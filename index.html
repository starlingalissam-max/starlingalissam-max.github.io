Customer Research & Business Development

ReadyGroup: customer discovery, product strategy and marketing experiments for a group trip coordination tool

ReadyGroup began as a business concept in my MBA program. I’ve since built an early MVP to test it; it hasn’t been validated with paying customers.

20+
customer interviews
30+
potential users engaged
Interviews, discovery conversations, prototype feedback
Methods
Early MVP; willingness to pay not yet tested
Stage

Problem

When a group travels together, one person usually ends up chasing everyone else.

The organizer collects responses, tracks who owes money, coordinates who is handling what, and sometimes fronts costs when people pay late or cancel. Most of that happens across scattered group chats. I wanted to know whether one place for RSVPs, responsibilities, payments, reminders and overall trip readiness would actually help.

My contributions

  • Conducted customer interviews and discovery conversations: 20+ interviews, 30+ potential users engaged
  • Synthesized the feedback into three recurring themes
  • Turned those findings into product priorities and scope
  • Built an early MVP and set up its $12-per-trip pricing (payments in test mode)
  • Planned the next marketing experiments: a pricing test and a Meta lead-ad campaign

Customer discovery

Instead of assuming what customers wanted, I started with conversations.

  • Customer interviews to understand how people experience the problem today
  • Discovery conversations about how organizers coordinate trips now and the tools they already use, especially group chats
  • Prototype feedback to see how organizers reacted to the concept as I refined it

Across the interviews, I separated what organizers mentioned once from what kept coming up. Three themes held up.

1

Payments and responses are recurring pain points.

Tracking who has paid and getting people to respond consistently came up as some of the most frustrating parts of organizing.

2

Group chats are good for conversation, poor as a source of truth.

Important details, responsibilities, payments and reminders get buried in them.

3

Organizers want help with follow-through, not another planning app.

They weren’t asking for a more complicated itinerary or travel-planning tool. They wanted help making sure things actually got done.

Product strategy

The research set the product’s priorities and its scope.

What I heardWhat I decided
Payments and responses are recurring pain pointsMade payment status and RSVP tracking central to the product instead of secondary features
Group chats bury important detailsOne board that shows the group’s overall readiness, with what still needs chasing at the top
Organizers want follow-through, not more planningKept ReadyGroup a lightweight coordination board, and left out booking, chat and full trip planning

Pricing model. The core board is free. Organizer features, payment tracking and reminders, cost $12 per trip, paid once by the organizer.

Marketing experiments

The research showed the problem is real for the organizers I spoke with. It hasn’t yet shown that they’ll pay to solve it, so that’s what I’m testing next.

A

Pricing test

Will organizers pay $12 per trip for payment tracking and reminders? The checkout is built and still in test mode.

B

Demand test

Planned: a Meta lead-ad campaign to gauge interest from group trip organizers before a wider launch.

No results yet. I’ll update this section when there’s data.

What this shows

  • Customer discovery: running conversations that uncover real problems rather than confirm what I already believed
  • Research into strategy: using what people said to decide what to build, and what to leave out
  • Testing assumptions: being clear about what has been validated and what still needs testing

Crescordia Business Strategy Case Study

Competitive analysis and strategic evaluation for a medical technology company

This was an academic case analysis completed for coursework, not a consulting engagement.

Medical technology
Industry
Five Forces, VRIO, SWOT, decision matrix
Frameworks
4
strategic options evaluated
25
top score: scientific testing

Business challenge

Crescordia faced pressure on product quality, innovation, competition, and the direction of its future products.

The question was which strategy would best improve its position over the long term.

My contributions

  • Assessed the industry with Porter’s Five Forces and the company with VRIO and SWOT
  • Proposed a fourth strategy, scientific testing for innovative material, alongside the case’s options
  • Built and scored a decision matrix comparing all four options on seven criteria
  • Wrote the final recommendation

Competitive analysis

I looked at the company from the industry level down to its own resources:

  • Porter’s Five Forces to assess competitive pressure in the industry
  • VRIO to identify which resources could support a lasting advantage
  • SWOT to summarize internal strengths and weaknesses alongside external opportunities and threats
  • Competitive analysis to compare Crescordia against others in its market

Strategic evaluation

I evaluated four strategies against seven equally weighted criteria: reputation, implementation cost, competitive advantage, market share, customer satisfaction, product quality, and future sales.

Each score is signed: a plus for a positive impact on the company’s future and a minus for a negative one, rated 1 to 5 by how large the impact is. A strategy’s total is the sum of its seven scores.

Decision matrix · primary analysis

How the four strategies scored

Scientifically Test for Innovative Material earned the highest total, 25, against 13 for the next-best option. Testing takes time and money, so it scored −2 on implementation cost, but it rated +5 on reputation, competitive advantage and future sales, and +4 on market share, customer satisfaction and product quality. That made it the strongest route to the differentiation Crescordia needed, and it became my recommendation.

Recommendation

Invest in scientific testing and R&D for innovative resorbable materials.

It scored 25 in the decision matrix, well ahead of the next option at 13, and it addressed the company’s core issue: improving product quality while building differentiation that could be harder for competitors to copy.

Trade-offs I weighed. These were academic judgments in my report, not tested outcomes:

  • Testing costs: scientific testing and clinical trials would cost money, and the number of trials needed was unknown, which is why it scored −2 on implementation cost.
  • Uncertain timelines: there was no firm end date for testing, and protecting Crescordia’s premium reputation during a long testing period would be a challenge.
  • Product quality: independent scientific testing could show that the products work and support a high quality standard.
  • Competitive differentiation: tested, proven materials could set Crescordia apart, though I expected competitors to try to copy the approach or compete on price.

Because this was an academic case, these benefits are projected rather than measured: better product quality, stronger differentiation, and long-term competitive positioning.

What this shows

  • Competitive analysis: assessing an industry and a company’s position with established frameworks
  • Structured evaluation: comparing every option against the same criteria instead of arguing for a favorite
  • Weighing trade-offs: recommending an option with a real cost because it was strongest overall

Retail Industry Financial Analysis

Financial ratio analysis and comparative research: Walmart against its competitors and the industry

A team project analyzing five retailers. Walmart was my assigned company: the Walmart analysis below is my work, and the overall comparison reflects the team’s combined report. All figures are as reported in the Spring 2023 assignment and have not been independently verified.

5

Walmart Inc.

Objective

Compare five major retailers, measure each against competitors and industry averages, and find the trends, risks, and opportunities.

Companies: Walmart, Nordstrom, TJ Maxx, Gap, and Target. The team worked from financial statements, industry benchmarks, competitor comparisons, and earnings call research.

My contributions

  • Prepared Walmart’s financial summary, comparing 2022 results with 2021
  • Researched industry ratios and compared Walmart’s quick ratio, profit margin and capital intensity with Dollar Tree, Target and an industry average
  • Analyzed Walmart’s Q4 2022 earnings call
  • Contributed the Walmart analysis to the team’s five-company report

Business performance

Walmart’s results year over year: revenue, EBIT, net income, assets, debt, cash flow and diluted EPS.

Ratio analysis

Walmart’s ratios as calculated in the report, benchmarked against Dollar Tree, Target and the industry average used in the project:

Key takeaways

From the report’s analysis:

1

Liquidity was a strength.

A quick ratio of 1.54 pointed to stronger short-term liquidity than the comparison companies in the report.

2

Margins were thin.

A 2.39% profit margin sat below the industry average used in the project.

3

Assets were used efficiently.

A capital intensity ratio of 0.43 suggested relatively efficient asset utilization against the industry benchmark.

4

The earnings call added context.

Walmart’s Q4 2022 call focused on general merchandise, pricing and customer value, and the effects of inflation on the business.

What this shows

  • Financial ratio analysis: calculating and interpreting liquidity, profitability and asset-use ratios
  • Comparative research: reading a company’s numbers against competitors and an industry average
  • Performance evaluation: pairing the numbers with management’s own commentary from the earnings call
